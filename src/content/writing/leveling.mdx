Music producers interact daily with a myriad representations of loudness. One work session can touch a mixer fader to lower an instrument’s volume, an analog VU meter to monitor a compressor’s output, a sound level meter for speaker calibration, and a digital LUFS gauge for mastering.

In a world with dozens of units and tools to use in different contexts and remember the specifics of, I’ve found in my online research an incredible amount of misinformation, assumptions about what “everyone” knows, and plain falsehoods. Lacking a comprehensive taxonomy of loudness units, meters, and their application, this is my attempt to create one.

## Units

Every meter, fader, and readout needs a scale. Different scales are useful in different contexts, so we have a lot of units, many of which are frequently conflated.

As you likely know, the basic unit of volume is the decibel (dB). It’s a relative unit by definition, expressing the ratio between two values. Decibels are useful for expressing the difference between two levels (“the snare is 3db quieter than the kick”), but to discuss the absolute level of a signal traveling through some medium — whether vibrating air, voltage through a wire, or bits in a DAW — we need new units. Many of these are defined in terms of decibels with a fixed reference point, where “0 [Unit]” is that reference point, and positive and negative values represent the ratio in decibels to that point.

### Physical Air Movement

What does it mean to measure the volume of audio? The most relevant to human experience is the travel of vibrations through air, the basis of our sense of hearing.

#### dBSPL: 20 μPa RMS in Air

A measurement of air displacement. In air, 0 dBSPL is defined as 20 μPa RMS, close to the smallest pressure a human ear can detect as sound. (The reference point is different in other fluids like water.)

> **What, and why, is RMS?**
>
> Given an audio signal, it's possible to get the instantaneous level at a specific point in time. However, this is rarely useful for assessing the *power* or *energy* of the signal. For that, a larger section of the signal must be considered.
>
> RMS, or root mean square, is how you average out an audio signal: you take the values at each point in time, square each one, add them all together, and take the square root of that sum.
>
> <img style='max-width: 480px;' src='https://doggo.ninja/GtNytf.png' />
>
> Why not just average them without the root and square part? That gives you something that follows the trends of the signal's amplitude, but it doesn't give you the *power* of the signal as [defined by physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_(physics)), which is more useful for assessing how audio affects us and our music.
>
> With limited exceptions like peak meters, when you see a dB measurement, you can safely assume it's based on the RMS of the signal calculated over some period of time.

#### Phons

The phon is rarely used when discussing loudness in the context of audio engineering because it's a subjective unit by nature; psychoacoustics research is the primary realm in which it's used.

The loudness of a sound in phons is defined as the level in dBSPL of a 1000 Hz sine wave that has the same perceived volume. For example, a sound is 20 phons if it sounds the same volume as a 1000 Hz sine tone at 20 dBSPL. A 500 Hz sine wave at 20 dBSPL will probably have a volume less than 20 phons because different frequencies are perceived differently by the human ear and brain.

Why is this unit rarely used in audio engineering? It can't be objectively calculated. While the relative volumes of different pure sine tones are pretty well studied, the loudness of sounds any more complex than that is preceived differently person to person. In academic contexts, the volume of a sound in phons is often calculated by surveying lots of people and averaging their answers.

#### dBA

When discussing the volume of sounds heard by humans, like when discussing noise pollution or hearing damage from concerts, raw dBSPL is rarely used because it's neither an effective proxy for hearing damage nor for perceived loudness. Instead, dBA is typically the unit of choice, applying a frequency-dependent weighting curve to dBSPL. If you see just "dB" used in the context of the volume of audible sounds, it's automatically implied to mean dBA.

The A-weighting curve was designed to approximate the inverse of a 40-phon equal-loudness contour (the dBSPL of 40-phon sine waves across the frequency spectrum). It also correlates well with the relationship between sound level meter microphone response and human hearing damage, which led to dBA's widespread use in regulatory contexts.

<img style='max-width: 350px;' src='https://doggo.ninja/lfxIDO.jpg' />

Many large concert venues have limits on maximum volume that the mixing engineer must follow. These are usually specified in dBA.

#### dBC

Occasionally, dBC is used as an alternative to dBA. It's sometimes written as dBc, which is incorrect and also confusing because dBc is [a real unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DBc) used in telecommunications that means something different.

The dBC unit represents C-weighted dBSPL. While the A-weighting curve follows an inverted 40-phon equal-loudness contour, the C-weighting curve is based on an inverted 100-phon contour. With a flatter curve including more bass, C-weighting better approximates the perceived volume of much louder sounds like those at concerts. IEC class 1 sound level meters are required to support dBC.

#### dBZ

You might see this unit used sometime. The "Z" means Z-weighted, which means… zero-weighted. If it's not weighted, how is this unit different from dBSPL? dBZ is defined with a specific frequency range that it has a flat response over and a specific error tolerance. This specificity matters in some industries.

[A different dBZ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DBZ_(meteorology)) is also used to represent reflectivity in weather radars, but if you're reading this article, that's probably not relevant to you.

#### dBB/dBD

These two weighting curves largely aren't used anymore. We will talk about LUFS later; its K-weighting curve is a descendant of the old B-weighting curve. The D-weighting curve was designed for aircraft noise.

#### Sones

While decibel-based scales are extremely useful to audio engineers and regualators alike for measuring the absolute volume of audio and modifying it in consistent, relative increments, psychoacoustics discussions require a unit that can intuitively represent ratios. The sone was created to be that unit; a 2-sone vacuum cleaner is half the volume of a 4-sone one.

Sones are defined in terms of phons, where 1 sone = 40 phons, and after that the loudness in sones doubles for each increase of 10 phons. Below 40 phons, the relative volume of sounds is less consistent, so different, specialized formulas are used based on our knowledge of psychoacoustics.

For similar reasons to phons, sones largely aren't used in the context of audio engineering or production because they're subjective units that can't be converted to other unit systems or measured.

### Analog Audio

Sound needs to get to your ears somehow, so we have speakers that vibrate the air in response to electricity. Voltage is a measure of the electrical potential between two points in a circuit; 0 volts sent into a speaker means there's no electrical pressure and is generally the center of the audio signal, and positive and negative voltages move the speaker diaphragm in opposing directions.

Everything from radio broadcasts to patch cables lives in this realm where amount of voltage determines wave amplitude, and until digital computers revolutionized audio, this was the only system that was relevant. While these units aren't directly used inside the digital world,  still paramount for getting audio into and out of your computer.

#### dBW: 1 W

The watt is a measurement of electrical power. The decib

Not really used in audio production contexts but you will see it in the context of speaker and amplification power.

#### dBm: 1 mW

Often erroneously used to mean 0.775 V, because old telephone systems ran at 600 Ω and if you do the math you get that voltage. Instead, dBu is the correct unit to use.

Since power = V²/R, the voltage is squared in the calculation. That means that (at 600 Ω) both +0.775 V and -0.775 V represent the same power level of 0 dBm.

#### dBu/dBv: 0.775 V RMS

Instead of using dBm incorrectly, you can use dBu, which is actually defined as the RMS voltage that would dissipate 1 mW in a 600 Ω load — in other words, 0.775 V.

The original name for dBu was dBv. It's no longer used because it confusingly conflicts with dBV. You might see dBv written when dBV is actually meant.

#### dBV: 1 V

It's a bit silly to use 0.775 V as a reference, so lots of people are moving to dBV which is simply defined as relative to 1 V. Can be RMS or peak, depending on the context, but by default you should assume it means RMS.

#### VU/dBVU: Sometimes +4&nbsp;dBu

The VU unit is a bit weirder than the others here, since it refers to the reading on an VU meter — archetypically, an analog device — and not a fixed reference value. While VU meters can be set to different target levels, they're traditionally set to a reference of +4 dBu and have an integration time (the window of time to smooth over) of 300 ms. The screen readouts are on a logarithmic scale, so VU is another decibel unit; you'll even sometimes see dBVU used.

VU meters have a specific physical response, usually called their *ballistic response*, with unique attack and decay characteristics responding to changes over their integration time. They approximate but don't precisely match either simple averaging or true RMS measurement of power, although they are proportionally closer to a flat average than RMS. Many people think VU means RMS; traditionally, it does not.

Many DAW plugins are available that simulate analog VU meters. Of course, it's not possible to set the reference value in dBu because there is no voltage in digital audio, so the calibration setting is often set in dBFS RMS. What value is used, then? Typically the dBFS value corresponding to +4 dBu, which depends on your preferred digital-to-analog alignment level. Conventions for this vary by country and standard (film vs. broadcast vs. CD) between -12 dBFS and -20 dBFS. What's dBFS? Glad you asked!

### Digital Audio

#### dBFS/dBO/dBov

Depends on the digital audio format, but refers to the maximum representable amplitude. For example, in floating-point formats, 0 dBFS is 1.0/-1.0.

Interestingly, that means you can actually go greater than 0 dBFS without clipping, which is why floating-point audio is typically discussed as having better headroom than fixed-point audio, which clips above 0 dBFS. This also means it's generally completely fine to go above 0 dBFS in floating-point formats — it might be uglier in waveform displays, but until you're converting to a non-floating-point format (like playing it through your speakers, or directly converting to fixed-point) you won't lose information and can always scale it down.

When DAWs display dBFS RMS meters, the calculation is typically done over a period of 300 ms. It's not a coincidence that this is the same as the integration period of traditional analog VU meters!

While dBFS peak is well-defined, dbFS RMS can be more ambiguous. Sometimes dbFS refers to the mathematical definition of RMS, where the RMS of a full-scale square wave is 0 dBFS and the RMS of a full-scale sine wave is around -3 dBFS. However, the AES17 standard for audio engineering dictates that dbFS RMS should actually have a built-in 3 dB offset so a full-scale sine wave is 0 dBFS and the square wave is +3 dBFS. I don't have a good answer for you on how to tell which is being used, but in my experience, the +3 dB system is more common in physical equipment, DAW faders, and spec sheets.

The units dBO and dBov are rarely used, but when they are, they refer to the standard where RMS follows the mathematical definition with no offset (in other words, a full-scale square wave is 0 dBov).

#### dBTP

Maybe you mastered your track and the audio never peaks above 0 dBFS. That means you can encode your audio without any single sample clipping. However, reconstructing a continuous (or just supersampled) waveform from these discrete samples — whether for a compression codec or for digital-analog-conversion before playback — can yield a waveform with "inter-sample peaks" louder than the samples themselves. Tools that can accurately calculate the volume of these inter-sample peaks use dBTP.

Some DAW meters will say they're dBFS but actually supersample to provide something closer to dBTP. For example, I noticed that Ableton Live 10 renders dBTP in the visual meter but dBFS in the peak value display. After upgrading to Ableton Live 12, the peak value display now also displays dBTP.

Why do these inter-sample peaks occur? You likely know that the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem tells us that, given a sample rate greater than the maximum frequency of a signal, you can always perfectly reconstruct the original signal. However, this doesn't guarantee that there won't be peaks in the original signal that are higher than the highest discrete samples. Perfect reconstruction of the discrete signal can be achieved with Whittaker-Shannon interpolation (also known as sinc interpolation), which is achieved in many DACs by applying a low-pass filter to the stepwise samples. It then follows that if the original signal had peaks louder than the resultant samples, then reconstructing those original signals will also reconstruct the peaks; therefore, inter-sample peaks are possible and dBTP matters.

Another example that I found intuitive is the Gibbs phenomenon caused by band limiting (necessary for digital audio due to Nyquist-Shannon) discontinuous signals like square waves.

#### LUFS/LKFS/dBK

The International Telecommunication Union, an agency of the United Nations, defined LKFS to provide a good unit for measuring the perceived loudness of digital audio, especially for broadcast normalization. An alternate name, LUFS, was later introduced. LUFS is one of the most important units of loudness to understand nowadays because all streaming and on-demand video services including Spotify, Apple Music, and YouTube normalize tracks and videos based on their overall LUFS volume.

LUFS is dBFS but compensated for the K-weighting curve, which is a standardized model that does a much better job at matching how humans perceive loudness at different frequencies than either A-weighting or C-weighting. LUFS is based on an RMS measurement without the +3 dB offset, and the algorithms for calculating it for different channel configurations (for example, surround sound) is highly standardized. LUFS is also gated: chunks of audio below a volume provided by the specification are ignored for a better representation of the loudness of the audio.

Overall, LUFS is a holistic standard for specifying the perceived loudness of a piece of audio content, and it's crucial to understand when distributing your audio. When mixing for broadcast contexts, you must adhere to strict LUFS limits. When mixing for music streaming releases, each platform has an LUFS target that your music will be scaled to hit — that means if you don't use your dynamic range well, your music can end up being scaled down to sound quieter and lower-quality than other songs. Even when you're not distributing to a platform that cares about LUFS, it's plain useful to know how loud your song sounds!

Rarely, dBK is used. It means the same thing as LUFS but it's weird, nonstandard, and super uncommon.

#### LU

You might see LU used as a relative unit when discussing the world of LUFS. Just like you might say something at -12 dB is 2 dB louder than -14 dBm, you can say that something -8 LUFS is 3 LU louder than -11 LUFS.

#### dBr

Some documentation, plugin manuals, and papers might mention dBr. This unit means decibels relative to *some reference*, which is probably specified by your plugin settings or the context of what you're reading.

## Leveling in the Real World

Now that we have a grasp of the insane system of units we use in the audio world, we can begin to examine the most common standards for audio leveling in the real world.

### Line Level

Audio devices that connect to each other benefit from a common reference level to simplify leveling and compatibility. The standard line level for consumer audio devices like CD players and home stereo systems is -10 dBV. It's different for professional audio at +4 dBu. Don't you love how it's a different unit?

It's useful to keep this in mind when connecting consumer audio devices to professional equipment — you might have to boost the level more than expected, and noise begins to be a concern.

By the way, the line level for professional audio is also why, as mentioned earlier, +4 dBu is the standard for VU meters.

### Alignment Levels

Until now, we've had two separate worlds: the digital world where dBFS, DAWs, and effect plugins power your workflows, and the analog world, where dBu, line level devices, and speaker systems let you bring your sounds to the real world. At some point though, you might want a way to relate your digital levels to analog levels, and so it's probably a good idea to pick a fixed reference point between dBFS and dBu. This is an example of an "alignment level," a defined anchor point across a signal chain.

Having such a conversion on hand means when working in your digital world, each volume corresponds to an analog voltage in a way that's consistent throughout your entire recording, production, mixing, and mastering process. You can use this reference when recording line in audio from your keyboard. You can use it when you need to calibrate your digital VU meter. If you need to share your track with someone in the professional world or to play on a fancy calibrated audio setup, it'll have an expected level. Here's where I get opinionated: even if you're just going to work in your DAW and release straight to streaming or Soundcloud, picking an alignment level gives you plenty of headroom, a consistent framework to work inside, and compatibility with a world built on decades of assumptions about audio engineering.

What alignment level should I use? Is there a standard choice across the globe? Of course not. But, at least in the United States where I live, SMPTE defined +4 dBu as -20 dBFS. This standard of -24 dBFS = 0 dBu (which would be -4 VU) is by far the most common among producers and audio engineers and matches the headroom that practically every piece of professional analog equipment can handle. While I recommend using that, it's worth noting that you will often come across -18 dBFS = 0 dBu used, which is the European standard set by EBU R 68. 6dB less headroom.

### Looking Backward: K-system

In 2000, at the peak of the [loudness wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loudness_war), mixing engineer Bob Katz had enough and published a proposal for a solution to the problem: a standard set of levels and headroom for mixing called the K-system (no relation to K-weighting). The system had three configurations: K-20, K-14, and K-12.

The K-system had you put a VU meter on your master track and use a sound level meter to set your speaker gain so 0 VU = 85 dBSPL at the listener's position. The only difference between the different configurations is the setting of the VU meter and therefore the headroom. K-20 had the VU meter set to -20 dBFS, K-14 was -14 dBFS, and, as I'm sure you can guess, K-12 was -12 dBFS.

Audio engineering has progressed a lot since then and, while it's useful to know about the K-system and some modern DAWs like Bitwig have support for it, Bob Katz himself has said that it's largely obsolete. After all, why unnecessarily limit your headroom or tie yourself to a VU meter?

### Looking Forward: EBU R 128 and ATCS A/85

10 years after Katz introduced the K-system, he joined a committee to create EBU R 128, an international standard for the loudness normalization of audio. R 128 requires a target loudness of -23 LUFS (± 0.5 LU) and a maximum true peak of -1 dBTP. "EBU mode" meters use units of LU where 0 LU = -23 LUFS.

In the United States and Canada, the typically followed standard is ATSC A/85, which dictates a target loudness of -24 LUFS (± 2 LU) and a maximum true peak of -2 dBTP. You might notice that these numbers are very similar, and something mixed for R 128 will always also be compliant with A/85 as long as the true peak is okay.

Both of these standards are very relevant to broadcast and cinema, but much less so for music. However, you might notice that the target numbers are very similar to where you'll likely already end up at when mastering at -20 dBFS = +4 dBu = 0 VU, which is going to be close to the EBU standard of -18 dBFS = 0 dBu = -4 VU as well. That makes these very easy standards to accidentally follow, and if you're just making music or other audio for digital distribution, you probably shouldn't ever care or think about them again!

The only part missing is what you set your speakers to. The best answer is whatever sounds good to your ears. The standard for cinema is still typically +4 dBu = 85 dBC, and as low as +4 dBu = 73 dBC is common for music production.

## Conclusion and Application

By now, you know there are a lot of choices, and you should be well-equipped to make them yourself. You will know a mix is good if you use a unique tool called "your ears" to tell if it's good.

However, if you want prescriptivism, here is some of that:

* Just mix at -20 dBFS. This doesn't mean your output is expected to *peak* at -20 dBFS — that would be much too quiet. Your peaks can and should be a lot higher, around -12 dBFS is typical but it depends a lot on your genre. If you're super European, -14 dBFS is fine
* If you're using a VU meter, calibrate it so -20 dBFS = 0 VU. You can also target -23 LUFS for mastering.
* It's totally fine to run a bit hot. Lots of amazing musicians don't pay attention to this at all and still make great music.
* If you care about calibrating your speaker volume, calibrate them one channel at a time so band-limited pink noise at +4 dBu (-20 dBFS RMS or 0 VU, here's some [test files](https://media.kognise.dev/)) is anything between 73 dBC and 85 dBC. You probably don't need to shill out for a sound level meter, it turns out an app on your phone is almost as accurate. Your ears are pretty good, too.
* When mastering for louder volumes (for example, Spotify's target is -14 LUFS, CDs can be pushed to like -10 LUFS) you'll probably need to bump your gain about 10-15 dB. Depending on how peaky your mix is, this is what limiters is for.
* When mastering for louder volumes (for example, Spotify's target is -14 LUFS, CDs can be pushed to like -10 LUFS) you'll probably need to bump your gain about 10-15 dB. Depending on how peaky your mix is, this is what limiters are for.
